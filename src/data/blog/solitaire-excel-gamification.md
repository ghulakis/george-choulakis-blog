Solitaire gives you something many work systems struggle to provide: 
clear rules, fast feedback and visible progress.

Solitaire and Excel have a strange thing in common.

You make a move and the system answers straight away. A card fits or it does not. A formula works or it throws an error. A total reconciles. A cell turns green. You always have some idea of where you stand.

That small feedback loop helps explain why a simple card game works so well as a short break, and why some people can spend hours inside a spreadsheet without feeling bored.

## What gamification means

Gamification means using ideas from games in everyday activities.

You have probably used it already:

- A progress bar showing that your profile is 80% complete
- A language app that tracks a streak
- A training platform that unlocks the next challenge
- A service portal that shows Step 3 of 6 and what you still need to do

These features give you a goal, show the result of an action and make progress visible. That can make an activity easier to follow and easier to continue.

> THE BASIC LOOP
>
> Challenge → Action → Feedback → Progress → Completion

## The strange Excel connection

Excel already contains many of the same ingredients.

You start with a problem. You try a formula. The result appears immediately. If it is wrong, you change something and try again. When the numbers finally reconcile, you get a tiny moment of completion.

- Formulas give immediate feedback
- Conditional formatting makes progress visible
- Errors create small problems to solve
- Undo makes experimentation cheap
- More advanced tools feel like new abilities you learn over time

This may help explain why spreadsheet work can become surprisingly absorbing for some people. The cells are only the interface. The satisfying part is the repeated cycle of problem, attempt, result and correction.

> SAME PATTERN, DIFFERENT SCREEN
>
> Solitaire: make a move → see the result → continue  
> Excel: change a formula → see the result → continue

## What the research actually says

Research on gamification gives us a useful warning: the design matters more than simply adding points.

<div class="effect-guide">
  <div class="effect-guide-title">How to read Hedges' g</div>
  <p>Hedges' g measures how large the difference is between two groups. A common rule of thumb is:</p>
  <div class="effect-scale">
    <span><strong>0.20</strong><small>small</small></span>
    <span><strong>0.50</strong><small>medium</small></span>
    <span><strong>0.80</strong><small>large</small></span>
  </div>
  <p class="effect-note">These are guides, not hard boundaries. Context and study design still matter.</p>
</div>

<div class="research-grid">
  <div class="research-card">
    <div class="research-title">Sailer & Homner, 2020</div>
    <p>This meta-analysis combined results from many gamified learning studies. It found positive effects on cognitive learning, motivation and behavior.</p>
    <p>For cognitive learning, Hedges' g was about <strong>0.49</strong>, which is close to the conventional medium-effect threshold. Results still varied with design and context. <a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-card">
    <div class="research-title">Bai, Hew & Huang, 2020</div>
    <p>They reviewed 30 interventions with 3,202 participants. Academic performance improved with Hedges' g = <strong>0.504</strong>, which falls in the conventional medium-effect range.</p>
    <p>Learners often valued clear goals and feedback. Some disliked gamification when it added pressure or little value. <a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-card">
    <div class="research-title">Sailer, Hense, Mayr & Mandl, 2017</div>
    <p>This experiment looked at specific game elements. Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs supported feelings of competence.</p>
    <p>Avatars, meaningful stories and teammates supported social relatedness. Different mechanics can influence people in different ways. <a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-card">
    <div class="research-title">Mekler et al., 2017</div>
    <p>Points, levels and leaderboards improved performance in an image-tagging task, but they did not increase intrinsic motivation or feelings of competence.</p>
    <p>Visible rewards can change behavior without making the activity itself more meaningful. <a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-card research-card-wide">
    <div class="research-title">Landers, 2014</div>
    <p>Landers' theory connects the findings above. Game elements can change attention, practice and persistence. Those changes can then affect learning outcomes.</p>
    <p>The mechanic itself does not create knowledge. <a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></p>
  </div>
</div>

## Why businesses should care

A lot of work gives people weak feedback. You finish a task, send it somewhere and wait. Progress disappears into email threads, systems and approvals.

A better interface can expose the progress that already exists.

> SAME TASK, CLEARER PROGRESS
>
> 37 / 200 complete  
> Accuracy: 99.2%  
> Next milestone: 50

The task has not changed, yet the employee can now see their progress.

The same idea works in onboarding and training. Give people a realistic situation, let them choose what to do and show the consequence. That creates a tighter learning loop than another long manual.

## Governments can use the same ideas

Public services often ask people to complete unfamiliar processes. A tax form, registration or document submission becomes easier when the system keeps answering two questions:

"Where am I?" and "What happens next?"

- Show how many steps are complete
- Explain which documents are still missing
- Use short scenarios for financial or digital literacy
- Use simulations in employee training

## Where gamification goes wrong

Game mechanics can also create bad incentives. Public leaderboards can add pressure. Rewards can make people chase the metric. Constant comparison can turn a productivity system into surveillance with a score attached.

> A PRACTICAL RULE
>
> Start with the behavior or learning problem. Choose a game element only when it helps with that problem.

## The bigger question

Information is easy to access. Keeping people engaged long enough to understand something, practice it and improve remains difficult.

Solitaire shows the value of a clear loop. Excel shows that the same loop can appear inside real work. Both give you fast feedback and a visible next move.

The useful question for designers is simple: what would happen if more work systems made progress this easy to see?
